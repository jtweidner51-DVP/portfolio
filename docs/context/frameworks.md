## Delivery Frameworks

**Lean Startup** is my default for innovation portfolios. At Shell Digital I embedded hypothesis-experiment-iterate across 50+ initiatives, accelerating time-to-MVP by 350% (52 to 15 days). The core discipline is defining the smallest experiment that tests the riskiest assumption. Most enterprise AI programmes fail because they skip this step and go straight to building.

**SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework)** I'm certified as both Agilist and Lean Portfolio Manager. I've used SAFe's portfolio governance layer extensively — particularly the Lean Portfolio Management component with Kanbanize at bp, aligning 8 strategic initiatives across 4 global regions. My honest take: SAFe's portfolio and strategy layers are genuinely useful at enterprise scale. The team-level prescriptions are often overkill. I pick and choose rather than dogmatically implementing the full framework.

**Scrum and Kanban** are my day-to-day delivery tools. At Shell I ran a flagship Scrum-at-scale transformation deploying to 105,000 global staff. At bp I used Kanban with WIP limits and flow metrics to make delivery health visible at portfolio level. I tend to reach for Kanban over Scrum when teams are doing operational work or handling unpredictable demand, and Scrum when they're building net-new products.

**Value Stream Mapping** is how I diagnose organisational bottlenecks before recommending structural changes. At bp I used it to surface $1.2M in annual savings for the China EV charging business and to identify the invisible fault rates at chargepoints that were driving customer churn. The value stream doesn't lie — it shows you where time and money actually go, which is often different from where leadership thinks they go.

**OKR Implementation** I've deployed OKR frameworks at portfolio level at both bp and Shell, always connected to strategic outcomes rather than activity metrics. The common failure mode I see is OKRs that measure output (features shipped, meetings held) rather than outcomes (cycle time reduced, decision quality improved). I align OKRs to business strategy first, then let teams define their own key results.

## Architecture / Technical Frameworks

**AI/ML Innovation Ecosystems** At Shell Digital I designed and scaled an Innovation Ecosystem spanning Deep Learning, Machine Vision, Natural Language Processing and Automated Reasoning, delivering a portfolio of 20+ production AI-enabled products. My role was governance and portfolio architecture rather than model building — ensuring the right problems were matched with the right AI approaches, managing the pipeline from experimentation to production, and killing initiatives that weren't demonstrating value fast enough.

**Cloud Migration (AWS/Azure)** I've led multiple cloud migration programmes: Shell's enterprise HR platform from on-premise to .NET on AWS serving 105,000 staff, and a $1.2M legacy-to-PaaS/SaaS transformation. My architectural instinct is pragmatic — cloud-first as a default, but with clear-eyed assessment of what should stay on-premise (latency-sensitive operational technology, regulatory constraints in energy).

**Data Platform Modernisation** At bp I implemented Lean Portfolio Management with data-driven governance using Kanbanize. I've worked with digital twins, predictive analytics and blockchain for emissions data exchange in the low-carbon portfolio. My current learning investment is in Python data engineering, RAG architectures and agentic AI workflows — building hands-on understanding of the stack so I can govern AI portfolios with authority rather than slides.

**MCP (Model Context Protocol)** is on my radar as the emerging integration standard for agentic AI. The Thoughtworks Technology Radar (Vol. 33, Nov 2025) has it at Trial, and it's dominating industry conversation around how agents interact with external tools and data sources. For AI portfolio leadership, understanding MCP's capabilities and limitations (security gaps, naive API-to-MCP conversion as an antipattern) is essential for governing responsible adoption.

## People & Organisation Frameworks

**Psychological Safety (Amy Edmondson)** is the foundation of everything I do with teams. At bp I achieved a 10% improvement in employee engagement scores through coaching interventions specifically targeting psychological safety. At Shell the 350% MVP acceleration was a direct outcome of removing fear of failure from the experimentation process. My practical application: leaders must model vulnerability first. When the most senior person in the room admits uncertainty, it gives everyone else permission to be honest.

**The Culture Map (Erin Meyer)** I use Meyer's eight dimensions (communication, feedback, decision-making, trust, scheduling, disagreeing, leading, persuading) to calibrate governance and stakeholder engagement across global teams. At Shell I designed adaptive governance for four hubs with fundamentally different cultural norms. At bp I ran a diagnostic Culture Map session with the M&T leadership team that surfaced collective blind spots about their own global operations — long-tenured UK members were confidently wrong about how colleagues in China and Bangalore worked. That reframing turned a political problem into a cognitive one.

**Facilitative Leadership / Mutual Learning Model (Roger Schwarz)** The 8 behaviours from Schwarz's model are my operating system for leadership without authority: state views and ask genuine questions, share reasoning and intent, test assumptions, focus on interests rather than positions, combine advocacy with inquiry, jointly design next steps, discuss undiscussable issues, and use decision-making rules that generate commitment. At bp I coached the M&T leadership team to adopt these as their team operating agreement. The practical test: 4 of the 7 VPs are still applying these behaviours in meetings I'm no longer in.

**Team Topologies (Skelton & Pais)** I've applied the stream-aligned team model in practice — redesigning bp's organisation from functional silos to 12 cross-functional value stream teams (45% reduction in decision-making bottlenecks). I'm aligned with the Thoughtworks Technology Radar's warning against capacity-driven development, where stream-aligned teams take on work from other streams when they have spare capacity. That's a local optimisation that compounds into delivery collapse.

**Coaching vs Mentoring** I distinguish between these deliberately. Coaching (asking questions that help someone find their own answers) is my default mode with executives. Mentoring (sharing experience and guidance) is what I use with emerging talent like Abdur at Shell, where I co-designed his development path from agility coach to product leadership. The choice depends on the person's readiness and the nature of the challenge.

## Strategy Frameworks

**Lean Portfolio Management** is my primary strategy execution framework. I've implemented it at enterprise scale at both Shell and bp using Kanbanize, connecting portfolio governance to OKRs and strategic outcomes. The key discipline is dynamic prioritisation — treating the portfolio as a living system, not a fixed annual plan. At bp this meant aligning 8 strategic initiatives across 4 global regions to net zero targets with continuous rebalancing.

**Wardley Mapping** I use Wardley Maps for technology strategy conversations, particularly when helping leaders understand where their technology investments sit on the evolution curve (genesis, custom, product, commodity). It's useful for cutting through vendor noise — if a capability is at the commodity stage, don't build it; buy it. If it's at genesis, don't outsource it; invest in understanding it.

**Design Thinking** I've used Design Thinking in workshop facilitation, particularly for the "Imagine 2030" strategic workshops I led for bp CEO's group (60 leaders) in Abu Dhabi. The diverge-converge-prototype rhythm works well for aligning senior leadership on innovation priorities when the problem space is ambiguous.

**Cynefin (Dave Snowden)** I use Cynefin to calibrate my delivery approach to the nature of the problem. Complex problems (most AI transformation challenges) need probe-sense-respond — safe-to-fail experiments, not detailed upfront planning. Complicated problems (cloud migrations, platform deployments) can tolerate more structured governance. The common failure I see is leaders applying complicated-domain thinking (expert analysis, best practice) to complex-domain problems (organisational change, AI adoption) where cause and effect are only visible in retrospect.

## Opinions & Takes

**"AI transformation failures are organisational, not technical"** — this is my core thesis. Every enterprise AI programme I've seen struggle had adequate technology. What they lacked was psychological safety for experimentation, cultural intelligence for global deployment, and facilitative governance that distributed ownership. The technology is the easy part.

**Agentic AI is overhyped at the application layer but underhyped at the governance layer.** Everyone's excited about what agents can do. Almost nobody is thinking seriously about how to govern a portfolio of autonomous AI agents at enterprise scale — security, accountability, cost management, toxic flow analysis. That's where the real leadership challenge sits, and it's where my experience in AI portfolio governance directly applies.

**SAFe is over-prescribed.** The portfolio and strategy layers are genuinely useful. The team-level ceremonies and roles are often cargo cult adoption — organisations implementing the rituals without understanding the principles. I'd rather see teams deeply understand flow, WIP limits and feedback loops than perfectly execute PI Planning.

**"Be Agile, don't just do Agile"** — I've been saying this for a decade and it's still the most common failure mode I see. Agile as a mindset (experimentation, learning, adaptation) transforms organisations. Agile as a process (standups, sprints, velocity) just adds meetings.

**Technical leaders who've stopped building are a liability.** Leaders making AI investment decisions without understanding what they're actually buying can't distinguish genuine capability from vendor noise. My decision to invest 500+ hours returning to hands-on coding wasn't nostalgia — it was risk management. I never want to be the executive who governs an AI portfolio through slides alone.

**The Thoughtworks Technology Radar's warning about "complacency with AI-generated code" resonates.** AI coding tools accelerate development but studies show code quality can decline over time as critical thinking atrophies. The answer isn't to avoid AI tools — it's to reinforce TDD, code review and quality practices precisely because the pace has increased. Speed without discipline is just faster technical debt.

**Communities of Practice are the most underrated organisational pattern.** At bp I established 3 global Networks of Excellence reaching 200+ practitioners with 40% adoption beyond mandate. Knowledge flows beat knowledge stocks every time. The Thoughtworks Technology Radar frames this as "knowledge flows over knowledge stocks" — and my experience confirms it. The best organisations invest in how knowledge moves, not just where it's stored.
