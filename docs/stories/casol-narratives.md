## Story 1: "Building from Zero — Shell Digital"

**Question this answers:** "Tell me about building something from scratch" / "How do you establish governance for AI programmes?" / "Describe your experience with Data & AI transformation"

**Context:** Recruited to establish Shell's first enterprise digital delivery practice from inception. No team, no governance, no delivery standards. 15+ VP-level stakeholders with competing priorities for AI investment and initial scepticism about whether agile delivery methods could work in a traditional engineering culture. The mandate was to operationalise advanced AI technologies (Deep Learning, Machine Vision, NLP, Automated Reasoning) across Shell's global operations.

**Action:** I designed adaptive governance across four global hubs (London, Amsterdam, Houston, Bangalore), each with fundamentally different cultural norms around decision-making, directness and trust. I used Erin Meyer's Culture Map to calibrate engagement approaches per hub rather than imposing a single operating model. For VP-level stakeholders, I facilitated strategic portfolio conversations where they reached their own conclusions about AI prioritisation, creating genuine ownership rather than compliance. When a PhD research team in Bangalore were dismissive of Agile, I flew there, spent time with them, then ran a session on the Socratic method — their own intellectual framework. I mapped agile practices onto hypothesis-experiment-iterate. The recognition was instant. They weren't resistant to the principles; they were resistant to having someone else's language imposed on their work.

**Stakeholder:** 15+ VP-level leaders across business units with competing AI investment priorities. 30-person global team built from scratch across four hubs. PhD research community in Bangalore who needed to be met where they were, not where I wanted them to be.

**Outcome:** $280M business savings in Year 1 (37% ahead of 3-year $2.7bn target). Team scaled 0 to 30 practitioners across 4 global hubs. Portfolio of 20+ AI-enabled production products delivered. Time-to-MVP accelerated 350% (52 to 15 days). Resistant research community converted to advocates through Socratic facilitation.

**Learning:** The $280M was an organisational achievement, not a technology one. Creating psychological safety for scientists to experiment, cultural intelligence to operate across four global hubs, and facilitative governance structures that let VPs genuinely own the portfolio — that's what unlocked the value. Imposed change fails; enabled change scales.

## Story 2: "Organisational Transformation — bp Low Carbon & Net Zero"

**Question this answers:** "How do you influence without authority?" / "Tell me about a complex organisational change" / "What's your position on sustainability?"

**Context:** Led enterprise-wide transformation across bp's low-carbon initiatives and Customer & Products organisation. VP-level stakeholders across 4 global regions with conflicting priorities and varying levels of transformation understanding. Functional silos creating decision-making bottlenecks. No direct line management authority over any of the teams. The programme was directly tied to CEO-level net zero commitments, making sustainable delivery non-negotiable.

**Action:** Every outcome was achieved without direct authority. I built consensus through facilitated workshops, transparent roadmapping, and quick wins that built confidence. When middle management resisted the redesign from functional silos to 12 cross-functional teams, I overcame it through pilot demonstrations, not executive mandates. I designed the OKR framework and Lean Portfolio Management system to accommodate different cultural interpretations of "autonomy" and "accountability" across four global regions. I aligned the portfolio explicitly to net zero OKRs, not just delivery milestones, and built governance frameworks that required sustainability considerations at programme level as a core delivery criterion.

**Stakeholder:** VP-level stakeholders across 4 global regions. bp CEO's leadership group (60 leaders at "Imagine 2030" workshops in Abu Dhabi). Cross-functional teams spanning technology, operations and policy domains. External technology partners coordinating delivery across internal teams.

**Outcome:** $1.2M annual savings for China EV charging operations. 45% reduction in decision-making bottlenecks. 40% improvement in delivery cycle time. 8 strategic initiatives aligned across 4 global regions to net zero targets. Contributed to approximately 400,000 tonnes of carbon reduction over three years. Strategic consultancy delivered to bp CEO's group (60 leaders).

**Learning:** The technology was the easy part. The real challenge was creating conditions where leaders across four regions, each with different cultural norms and competing priorities, could genuinely align around shared outcomes. Sustainable delivery wasn't optional in that portfolio — it was load-bearing.

## Story 3: "Executive Coaching Through Complexity — bp M&T Merger"

**Question this answers:** "How do you manage executive stakeholders?" / "Tell me about coaching senior leaders" / "Give me an example of your diagnostic approach"

**Context:** Selected to lead transformation delivery for bp's £780M Marketing & Transformation function during a critical organisational merger. 7+ SVP-level leaders making overconfident assumptions about delivery complexity and timeline feasibility. 8 merged business units, multiple external delivery partners, and no established governance across the new structure. The SVP asked me to facilitate a group charter session. I pushed back.

**Action:** I proposed 1:1 coaching sessions first. My instinct was that the team wasn't ready for group work, and I was right. What emerged was a critical hidden dynamic: two of the seven VPs had worked closely with the SVP for over a decade, forming what the others perceived as an "inner circle." The remaining VPs felt excluded from real decision-making — some were close to tears in their 1:1s. I then ran a Culture Map session with the team. Four UK members who'd been at bp for over a decade were clustered tightly together. I used China and Bangalore as test cases — they made confident observations about how colleagues in those hubs worked, and through the Meyer lens, they were badly wrong. A decade inside one culture had given them fluency and blind spots in equal measure. I used Schwarz's 8 facilitative leadership behaviours as the anchor for their team operating agreement, and the Culture Map gave them a non-threatening vocabulary for their blind spots.

**Stakeholder:** 7+ SVP-level leaders through merger complexity. The SVP who initially wanted a shortcut to a charter. VPs who felt excluded from an inner circle. 200+ practitioners across 3 global Networks of Excellence.

**Outcome:** Hidden team dysfunction diagnosed and addressed through 1:1 diagnostics before group work. Schwarz's 8 behaviours adopted as team operating framework. Enterprise Transformation Playbook deployed across 8 business units. 40% adoption beyond initial mandate. 10% improvement in employee engagement scores.

**Learning:** AI transformation failures are organisational, not technical. When the most senior person in the room admits uncertainty, it gives everyone else permission to be honest. The 1:1 diagnostic before group work is now my default approach — you can't build a charter on a fractured foundation. I also learned that long-tenured, culturally homogeneous leadership teams develop collective blind spots about their own global operations, and that naming those blind spots requires a framework, not a confrontation.

## Story 4: "People Development — Seeing Potential Others Miss"

**Question this answers:** "Tell me about developing someone on your team" / "How do you approach people leadership?" / "Give me an example of building a team"

**Context:** Two stories, two decades apart. At Shell Digital (2018-2021), Abdur was a skilled agility coach with capability but no clear path forward. At Logica CMG in South Wales (late 1990s), the company was struggling to recruit for a new document handling centre because they only considered graduates.

**Action:** At Shell, I saw something in Abdur that others hadn't — the potential to make the leap from facilitator to product owner and eventually product leadership. I created a deliberate development environment where he could fail small and learn fast, protected his space to grow while connecting him across the organisation, and co-designed his path with increasing responsibility while coaching through the transition. At Logica, I hired a nanny, a teacher, a classical musician, and a barman into a technology operation. None had technology backgrounds. I also recruited two PhD students struggling to break out of academia who had no IT background whatsoever.

**Stakeholder:** Abdur — who went from agility coach to Head of Product at Puro.Earth (climate tech). The Logica team — non-traditional hires who became high performers. Two PhD students who became brilliant business analysts whose research rigour was head and shoulders above the IT people's analytical abilities.

**Outcome:** Abdur is now Head of Product at Puro.Earth, a climate tech company tackling carbon removal. He wrote: "Janek empowers members of staff to become great versions of themselves." The non-traditional Logica hires turned out to be hardworking, loyal, and excellent at the work. The PhD students went on to have long, successful IT careers.

**Learning:** The best teams are built by seeing what people could become, not what they currently are. This isn't a management technique — it's a leadership conviction that has stayed consistent across 25 years. Cultivation over command.

## Story 5: "Values in Action — iSAW International Ambassador"

**Question this answers:** "Tell me about your values" / "What do you do outside corporate roles?" / "How do you approach inclusive leadership?"

**Context:** iSAW International advances gender equality and women's leadership in the workplace. They were at a critical inflection point — growing fast with a strong mission but a strategy that hadn't kept pace with their ambition. I was appointed Strategic Advisor, working pro-bono.

**Action:** I brought the same diagnostic rigour to iSAW that I apply to £100M transformation programmes. Understood their goals first, challenged their assumptions constructively, and helped them separate urgent from important. Facilitated the strategic development process from vision to prioritised roadmap — pro-bono, because the mission mattered. I treated inclusive leadership not as a values statement but as a delivery risk mitigation strategy: homogeneous teams miss the organisational resistance patterns that kill technically sound AI programmes.

**Stakeholder:** iSAW International CEO and leadership team. The broader community of women in leadership that iSAW serves.

**Outcome:** Appointed Ambassador to iSAW International — an active role, not a title. The CEO publicly recognised the contribution on LinkedIn, describing the work as "translating big ideas into actionable strategy." iSAW's strategic direction sharpened at a critical growth stage.

**Learning:** Facilitative leadership is who I am, not just what I do at work. The skills that work in a C-suite Programme Steering Committee work identically in a volunteer strategy session. Different context, same approach. And inclusive leadership isn't just the right thing to do — diverse diagnostic teams consistently produce better problem identification.

## Story 6: "Resilience — Calm and Carry On"

**Question this answers:** "Tell me about a time you showed resilience" / "How do you handle external shocks to a programme?" / "How do you manage a difficult senior stakeholder?"

**Context:** I was leading low-carbon transformation at bp when two things hit in quick succession. The SVP responsible for the EV business was openly dismissive of the leadership change needed to build an agile enterprise — he saw it as a distraction from commercial delivery. Then an overnight message arrived: pivot from EV network growth to EBITDA positive by year end. It was August. No context, just a cost-reduction call to arms. Bernard Looney's subsequent departure triggered a hard pivot back to hydrocarbons. Programmes I'd spent two years building were suddenly deprioritised overnight.

**Action:** I didn't push back on the EBITDA ask — I acknowledged it completely. But I knew the real problem wasn't cost; it was invisible fault rates at the chargepoints driving customer churn. I took two Lean Six Sigma specialists into the value streams, surfaced the empirical data, and reframed the conversation from cost-cutting to service quality as a commercial lever. On the hydrocarbon pivot, I worked with my teams to ringfence what was genuinely valuable and repackage it in language that fit the new strategic narrative. For the dismissive SVP, I brought him into a working session rather than a steering committee. I worked the problem from within the constraints of the system I was actually in, not the one I wished I was in.

**Stakeholder:** The dismissive SVP who eventually became an active sponsor. My own VP (who acknowledged the systemic issue but couldn't act on it). The delivery teams whose energy and momentum I needed to protect while absorbing pressure from above.

**Outcome:** The SVP eventually became an active sponsor — not because I convinced him ideologically, but because I met him where he was. The programme survived the hydrocarbon pivot leaner than before; some of the forced optimisations made us more disciplined about what actually mattered. The fault data I surfaced changed the conversation from cost-cutting to service quality.

**Learning:** Resilience isn't about staying the course — sometimes it's about reading when the course itself needs to change. I'd also get to the SVP's datapoints earlier next time: understanding what metrics his EVP was holding him to would have let me speak his language from day one. A rough data point earlier is worth more than a polished one later. In bp, escalating over your SVP isn't a neutral act — it's a career move. You work the problem from inside the constraints you actually have.

## Story 7: "The Optimism Tax — Learning & Reflection"

**Question this answers:** "What's a belief you've changed?" / "Tell me about learning from a mistake" / "Why are you moving on from bp?"

**Context:** When I joined bp, the conditions looked ideal — a CEO publicly committed to net zero, strong cultural signals around digital enablement, real appetite for transformation. I came in with high conviction and moved fast. Within two years, the strategic context had reversed in ways I genuinely didn't see coming. Bernard Looney's departure was overnight; it took everyone by surprise. The programmes I'd built around CEO-level strategic alignment lost their anchor simultaneously.

**Action:** I had to sit with an uncomfortable question: was my optimism an asset or a liability? I'd coached executives to challenge their own assumptions and here I was having failed to challenge mine. The signal I missed was structural: I was using the CEO's vision as a proxy for organisational stability. I hadn't stress-tested the assumption underneath — that the vision and the person were separable. They weren't. I shifted investment from programme outcomes to people capability — because executive coaching creates durable capability even when strategy shifts. I also returned to technical fundamentals: coding, AI architectures, RAG. Partly because I enjoy it; partly because it keeps my judgment sharp on what's actually possible when clients are being oversold.

**Stakeholder:** The executives I coached — 4 of the 7 VPs are actively applying Schwarz's facilitative behaviours in meetings I'm no longer in. Marketing and IT leaders who were locked in a tool selection battle until I ran one facilitated session using "interests not positions" — the conflict dissolved in forty minutes. Those leaders still use that lens.

**Outcome:** The last year at bp produced some of the most rewarding work despite constrained conditions. The executives are more capable than when I found them — that's durable regardless of what bp does next. My programmes delivered over 400,000 tonnes of carbon reduction in three years. The technical reskilling (500+ hours in Python, React, data engineering) gave me the foundation to assess AI capabilities with authority rather than hope.

**Learning:** The belief I've loosened is that organisational readiness follows strategic intent. I used to think optimism was a leadership superpower. It is — but only when it's stress-tested. Culture moves slower than strategy, and I've learned to treat that gap as the actual delivery problem. What I haven't let go of is that technology is a force multiplier for human intention — 400,000 tonnes of carbon reduction in three years. That's not nothing. There's more to do.
