# Prepared Interview Answers

## "Walk me through your background"

I'm a Computer Science graduate who's spent 30 years in technology — but the thread running through my career isn't technology itself, it's making technology work inside complex organisations.

I started on the supplier side. Twenty years at Logica, Siemens and Atos, where I cut my teeth on mission-critical operations — BBC iPlayer serving 124 million monthly users, 99.999% uptime, the kind of environment where failure isn't theoretical. That taught me operational discipline and what it actually means to deliver when the stakes are real.

Then Shell recruited me to build something from nothing. I founded their first enterprise digital delivery practice at Shell Digital — zero team, zero governance, zero delivery standards. Over three years I built a 30-person global team across London, Amsterdam, Houston and Bangalore, and we delivered $280M in AI-enabled business value in Year 1 against a three-year $2.7bn target. The portfolio spanned Deep Learning, Machine Vision, NLP and Automated Reasoning. That's where I learned that AI transformation failures are organisational, not technical — the technology was never the bottleneck. It was psychological safety, cultural intelligence across global hubs, and governance that let senior leaders genuinely own the portfolio rather than just approve it.

I moved to bp because the CEO had committed to net zero and I wanted to be in the room where that was being delivered. Four years across low-carbon growth engines — EV charging, hydrogen business transformation — then leading the agility transformation for the £780M Marketing & Transformation merger, coaching 7 SVP-level leaders through it. The programmes contributed to roughly 400,000 tonnes of carbon reduction. When the strategic context shifted after the CEO's departure, I learned some hard lessons about the gap between strategic intent and organisational readiness. I wrote about it internally — I called it "Organisational Zombies." Leadership acknowledged it and buried it. The irony was instructive.

Now I'm pivoting toward AI portfolio leadership. I've spent the last year investing 500+ hours back in hands-on technical work — Python, React, RAG architectures, data engineering — because I believe the leaders governing AI portfolios need to understand what they're actually governing. I built a RAG pipeline, built my portfolio website from scratch, and I'm using AI tools like Claude Code daily. Not to become a developer again, but to bridge the gap between strategic governance and technical reality with genuine authority.

## "Why are you interested in this type of role?"

Two reasons, one strategic and one personal.

Strategically, I've spent a decade building AI capabilities inside large organisations and I've watched the challenge shift. It's no longer "can we build AI?" — every enterprise can access the models. The question now is "how do we govern a portfolio of AI initiatives responsibly at scale?" That's portfolio governance, benefits realisation, stakeholder alignment, cultural change. It's exactly what I've been doing, just with the AI landscape evolving underneath it. I want to be where that challenge is sharpest.

Personally, I went back to bp because the CEO committed to net zero and I wanted to contribute to decarbonisation. That conviction hasn't changed — the planet is still on fire. What's changed is my understanding of where I can make the most impact. Inside a large energy company, I was constrained by the system I was in. In a role where I can bring diagnostic rigour, AI portfolio governance experience, and honest challenge to multiple organisations, the leverage is significantly higher. I also want to be somewhere that takes technology opinions seriously — that has the intellectual honesty to put things on "Hold" when the evidence says so, not just when it's commercially convenient.

## "What are your greatest strengths?"

**Diagnostic instinct.** I don't take problems at face value. When an SVP at bp asked me to facilitate a charter session for his leadership team, I pushed back. I did 1:1 coaching sessions first and uncovered a fractured team with an inner circle dynamic that nobody would have named in a group setting. Then I ran a Culture Map session and discovered the long-tenured UK core were confidently wrong about how their global colleagues operated. That diagnostic step — doing the work before the work — has consistently been the difference between interventions that stick and ones that look good in a deck but change nothing.

**Influencing without authority.** At bp I had no direct line management over the delivery teams or the SVP-level leaders I coached. Every outcome — the Transformation Playbook with 40% adoption beyond mandate, the 12 cross-functional teams, the 10% engagement improvement — was achieved through facilitation, evidence-based challenge and coalition building. I'm most effective when I have to earn influence rather than inherit it.

**Bridging strategy and technical reality.** I'm a Computer Science graduate who's spent a decade in executive coaching, now actively writing code again. I can sit in a C-suite governance forum and translate delivery risk into business language, then walk into a technical architecture discussion and know whether what I'm hearing is feasible or vendor noise. That dual fluency is rare, and it matters enormously when you're governing AI portfolios where the gap between promise and delivery is significant.

**Depth in modern AI/ML engineering.** I've governed AI portfolios at the strategic layer — Deep Learning, NLP, Machine Vision at Shell — but my hands-on understanding of the current stack (LLM fine-tuning, agentic architectures, RAG pipeline optimisation, inference infrastructure) is still developing. That's why I've invested 500+ hours in the last year on Python, data engineering and building a working RAG pipeline. I'm not trying to become a machine learning engineer. I'm also working through the implications of agentic AI governance — how portfolio management needs to evolve when your "products" are autonomous agents with tool access and decision-making capability. That's new territory for everyone.

## "Tell me about your leadership style"

I lead through trust, transparency and evidence — not authority. Across 20+ years I've rarely had direct line management over the people I've needed to influence.

My operating system draws on three interconnected frameworks. Edmondson's psychological safety research shapes how I build team environments — I deliberately create conditions for interpersonal risk-taking because AI transformation demands that teams experiment and fail without fear. Meyer's Culture Map shapes how I calibrate governance and engagement across global teams — the same transformation approach needs cultural adaptation between London, Houston, Bangalore and Abu Dhabi. And Schwarz's Mutual Learning Model provides my 8 facilitative behaviours for leadership: stating views while asking genuine questions, testing assumptions, focusing on interests rather than positions, and discussing the things that nobody wants to name in a group setting.

The practical test of my leadership style is what happens when I leave. At bp, 4 of the 7 VPs I coached are actively applying Schwarz's facilitative behaviours in meetings I'm no longer in. That's the residue I'm after — durable capability, not programme dependency.

One story captures it well. At Shell, a team of PhD researchers in Bangalore were dismissive of Agile. My line management in Amsterdam was frustrated. Instead of pushing harder, I flew to Bangalore, spent time with them, and ran a session on the Socratic method — their own intellectual framework. I mapped agile practices onto hypothesis-experiment-iterate. The recognition was instant. They weren't resistant to the principles; they were resistant to having someone else's language imposed on their work. That's the difference between imposed change and enabled change, and it's the difference I try to make every time.

## "Why are you leaving / why did you leave your current role?"

I joined bp because the CEO had committed to net zero and I wanted to contribute to that mission directly. And the work mattered — my programmes contributed to roughly 400,000 tonnes of carbon reduction over three years.

But I also learned something important about working inside large organisations: culture moves slower than strategy. When the strategic context shifted after the CEO's departure, I found myself coaching executives to challenge their assumptions while realising I hadn't challenged my own — I'd built my influence architecture on a single point of failure. The programmes I'd spent two years building were deprioritised overnight. That taught me that organisational readiness doesn't automatically follow strategic intent. It's a belief I've loosened, and I'm a better advisor for it.

The honest answer is that I've reached the limits of what I can achieve as an internal transformation leader in a large energy company. The protected leadership layers, the inability to have certain conversations, the system constraints — they're real. Moving to a role where I can bring the same diagnostic rigour, the same frameworks, the same honest challenge, but with the licence that comes from being external — that significantly increases my leverage and impact.

## "What does success look like to you in the first 90 days?"

**First 30 days: Listen and diagnose.** My instinct from the bp M&T merger is that you learn more from 1:1 conversations than from reading strategy documents. I'd want to understand the real dynamics — what's working, what's stuck, where the undiscussable issues are. I'd also want to understand the technical landscape with enough depth to have credible conversations, not just governance oversight.

**Days 30-60: Quick wins with visible impact.** Identify one or two delivery bottlenecks that are widely felt but haven't been addressed, and solve them. At bp, the first thing I did was surface hidden fault rates at EV chargepoints that were driving customer churn — a data-driven reframe that changed the conversation from cost-cutting to service quality. I'd look for an equivalent: a problem where evidence changes the narrative.

**Days 60-90: Establish the operating rhythm.** By this point I'd want to have co-created (not mandated) the governance frameworks, established the facilitated forums where honest delivery conversations happen, and started building the networks of practice that sustain capability beyond any single programme. The measure isn't whether I've delivered a big outcome yet — it's whether the conditions for sustained delivery are in place and the team trusts the approach.

The overall principle: earn credibility through listening and visible impact before trying to change anything structural.

## "Do you have any questions for us?"

**On the work itself:**
- What does the current AI/transformation portfolio look like, and where do you see the biggest governance gaps?
- What's the balance between strategic advisory work and hands-on delivery? I'm at my best when I can do both.
- What does "good" look like for someone in this role after 12 months? What would need to be true?

**On the organisation:**
- How does the leadership team handle disagreement? I'm interested in whether constructive challenge is genuinely welcomed or just described that way.
- What's the biggest transformation initiative that didn't work the way you expected? I learn a lot from how organisations talk about their failures.
- How do you balance the tension between pace and quality, particularly with AI-generated deliverables?

**On culture and development:**
- What does professional development look like here? I'm particularly interested in how technical depth is valued alongside strategic leadership capability.
- How do teams share knowledge across engagements? I've found that communities of practice and knowledge flows are the most underrated organisational pattern — I'm curious whether that resonates here.

## "Tell me about a time you failed"

The honest answer is the "optimism tax" I paid at bp. When I joined, the conditions looked ideal — CEO committed to net zero, real appetite for transformation. I came in with high conviction and moved fast. Within two years the strategic context had reversed overnight when Bernard Looney departed. The signal I missed was structural: I was using the CEO's vision as a proxy for organisational stability, and I hadn't stress-tested the assumption that the vision and the person were separable. They weren't. When he went, I lost my most powerful alignment tool simultaneously.

The belief I've loosened is that organisational readiness follows strategic intent. I used to think optimism was a leadership superpower. It is — but only when it's stress-tested. The real lesson: push hard, but build the humility to notice when the environment has changed before the environment forces you to notice it.

What I preserved was the people capability. Four of the seven VPs I coached are still applying Schwarz's facilitative behaviours in meetings I'm no longer in. One example: Marketing and IT were locked in a tool selection battle. I ran one facilitated session using "interests not positions." The conflict dissolved in forty minutes. Those leaders still use that lens. That's durable, regardless of what bp does strategically.

I want to be the person that organisations call when they need to make AI portfolios actually work — not the technology, but the organisational conditions around it. Psychological safety for experimentation. Cultural intelligence for global deployment. Governance that distributes ownership rather than centralises control.

Specifically, I see myself leading AI transformation portfolios at enterprise scale, combining strategic governance with enough technical depth to hold honest conversations about what's feasible and what's vendor noise. The hands-on technical investment I'm making now — Python, RAG architectures, agentic AI workflows — is deliberate positioning for a future where the line between strategic leader and technical practitioner continues to blur.

I also want to contribute to the conversation about sustainable AI. The carbon cost of AI workloads is a real and growing consideration, and the leaders governing these portfolios need to treat it as a first-class delivery criterion alongside cost, risk and strategic alignment. That's where my energy sector experience and Green Software Foundation certification connect.
